A Tulip Bulb Speculation, Holland · Winter 1636–1637

Before you begin

It is late November, 1636. Across the Dutch Republic, tulip bulbs — planted in the ground and invisible until spring — are being bought and sold on promises alone. Weavers, innkeepers, and merchants are turning fortunes trading paper contracts for flowers they will never see. You have 1,000 guilders (roughly three to six years' wages for a skilled craftsman) to speculate with.

Each week you may buy or sell contracts in four real tulip varieties. Prices will rise — dramatically. Somewhere ahead lies February 3rd, 1637. What happens that day is historical fact, not a twist I invented. Your job is to decide when to hold your nerve, and when to get out.

  • Prices move once per week when you click Advance the Calendar.
  • Buy or sell outright and you own the bulb — your risk is limited to what you paid.
  • Lock in a futures contract instead, and you pay only a small deposit now (this is the real historical "wind trade," or windhandel) — but at settlement you owe the full difference between the locked price and the market price, for better or worse. It's a way to control far more tulips than your cash alone allows, at far greater risk. You can put a maximum of 10 bulbs on credit this way per week.
  • On the night of the Haarlem auction — when the room famously fell silent — you can still buy, but you cannot sell. There were simply no buyers.
  • A short historical note appears each week — this bubble is a real, well-documented event.
  • Your goal: end the game with more guilders than you started. Most speculators didn't — and some ended up owing more than they had.

What Really Happened

On February 3rd, 1637, a routine bulb auction in Haarlem drew no bidders at all — buyers simply didn't show. Within days, confidence collapsed across the Dutch Republic and prices for even the finest bulbs fell to a small fraction of their peak. Courts largely refused to enforce the futures contracts that traders had signed, treating many of them more like gambling debts than binding sales. Historian Anne Goldgar's research later found that while individual traders were ruined, there's little evidence the crash caused the widespread bankruptcies or broader economic damage that the popular legend describes — the real story is smaller, and stranger, than the myth.

For Discussion

  1. What made tulip bulbs — rather than, say, bread or timber — the object of this speculative bubble?
  2. "Windhandel" trading meant buying and selling a bulb that stayed in the ground, unseen, all winter. How did that distance between the object and the trade encourage risk-taking?
  3. Compare this bubble to a more recent one (housing in 2008, dot-com stocks in 2000, a cryptocurrency of your choice). What's the same? What's different?
  4. Courts refused to enforce most tulip futures contracts after the crash. Who do you think that decision helped, and who did it hurt?
